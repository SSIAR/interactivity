How breathing may reach the nerve

You cannot instruct your heart to slow down, but you can change how you breathe. One proposed model suggests that this is the doorway. It comes from a narrative review and describes a plausible route; it has not been traced step by step in people practising SKY.

A slow, resisted breath

SKY opens with slow ujjayi breathing, about 2 to 4 breaths a minute, drawn against gentle resistance in the throat.

Sensors notice

Stretch and pressure sensors in the airways and lungs are thought to send more signals up the vagus nerve to the brainstem.

The brainstem replies

Signals return down the vagus to the heart, slowing its rate. This is the parasympathetic, rest-and-restore side of the nervous system.

The wider ripple

The review suggests the practice may also influence cortisol, prolactin and oxytocin, hormones tied to stress, resilience and bonding.

What the wider evidence reports

Eight studies have examined SKY and the vagus nerve. Most are small, and several have no comparison group, so read them as early signals that point the same way. The filled label marks randomised controlled trials, the most rigorous design here.

Six terms, in plain words

Heart rate variability (HRV)

The small variation in time between one heartbeat and the next. More variation generally signals a system that adapts well to stress.

Vagal tone

Vagus nerve activity that slows the heart rate.

Sympathetic activity

The nervous system response that prepares the body for fight or flight.

Parasympathetic activity

The nervous system response that promotes relaxation.

Sympathovagal balance

The balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic responses.

Autonomic function

The automatic body processes that run without conscious control, such as heartbeat and digestion.
